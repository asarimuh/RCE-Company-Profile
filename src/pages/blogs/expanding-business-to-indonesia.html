Indonesia is no longer an emerging market that savvy investors "keep an eye on." With 280 million people, the world's fourth-largest population, a rapidly expanding middle class, and one of the most active digital consumer bases in Asia, Indonesia has become a market that serious businesses cannot afford to ignore.

But entering Indonesia is not like flipping a switch. The country has a distinct regulatory environment, a layered bureaucratic process, and cultural nuances that have caught many well-resourced foreign companies off guard. This guide exists to change that — giving you a clear, complete, and honest picture of everything involved in making Indonesia your next market.

2 Legal Structure: PT PMA — The Only Option for Foreign Businesses

If you are a foreign company or individual looking to operate a business in Indonesia, there is essentially one primary legal structure available to you: the PT PMA — Perseroan Terbatas Penanaman Modal Asing, or Foreign Capital Investment Limited Liability Company.

Key Term

PT PMA (Foreign-Owned Limited Liability Company)

A PT PMA is the legal entity that allows foreign nationals and foreign companies to own and operate a business in Indonesia. It is a separate legal entity from its shareholders, meaning it can enter contracts, own assets, hire employees, and bear liabilities in its own name. Foreign ownership is permitted up to 100% in many sectors, though some industries have caps defined by the Negative Investment List (DNI).

It's important to understand how a PT PMA compares to other structures you might encounter when researching Indonesia:

Representative Office (KPPA)

  • Cannot conduct commercial or revenue-generating activities
  • Cannot sign sales contracts or issue invoices
  • Limited to market research, liaison, and coordination
  • Lower setup cost and simpler process
  • Suitable only for exploratory or pre-entry phase
  • Must eventually transition to PT PMA to operate commercially

PT PMA (Foreign-Owned Company)

  • Fully authorized to conduct commercial activities and generate revenue
  • Can sign contracts, issue invoices, and open corporate bank accounts
  • Can hire both local and expatriate employees
  • Can own property (in certain categories)
  • Subject to full tax and compliance obligations
  • The correct structure for any serious market entry

⚠️ Important

Operating commercially through a Representative Office — or using a local nominee to hold shares on your behalf — is illegal under Indonesian law and carries serious legal and financial penalties. Always establish a proper PT PMA before conducting any revenue-generating activity.

3 Capital & Ownership Requirements

Understanding the financial and ownership thresholds is critical before you begin the setup process. Getting these wrong at the planning stage can cause significant delays or force you to restructure later.

  • 💰

    Minimum Investment Requirement

    A PT PMA must have a total investment plan of at least IDR 10 billion (approximately USD 625,000) excluding land and buildings. This is the total planned investment — not the amount you need to deposit immediately. The paid-up capital requirement is IDR 2.5 billion (approximately USD 156,000).

  • 📊

    Foreign Ownership Cap by Sector

    Foreign ownership of up to 100% is permitted in many sectors. However, some industries have restrictions defined by Indonesia's Positive Investment List (replaced the old Negative Investment List). Sectors like media, telecommunications, banking, and certain retail categories have specific foreign ownership caps ranging from 49% to 67%. Always verify your specific KBLI code before assuming full ownership is permitted.

  • 🏢

    Registered Address Requirement

    Every PT PMA must have a registered legal address in Indonesia. A virtual office address is accepted for initial registration purposes in most regions, though certain business activities require a physical office verified by local government. Jakarta, Surabaya, and Bali are common choices for the registered address of newly entering foreign companies.

  • 👥

    Minimum Shareholder & Director Requirements

    A PT PMA requires a minimum of two shareholders (individuals or entities), one Director, and one Commissioner. Foreign nationals can serve as Director. At least one Commissioner is required, and in many structures, a local Indonesian Commissioner is recommended for smoother operational compliance — though it is not always legally mandatory.

  • 📋

    KBLI Business Classification Code

    Every PT PMA must specify its business activities using the KBLI (Klasifikasi Baku Lapangan Usaha Indonesia) code system — Indonesia's standard business activity classification. Choosing the wrong KBLI code can restrict the licenses you can obtain and the activities you're legally permitted to conduct. Multiple KBLI codes can be registered, but each must align with your actual intended operations.

4 Full Documentation Checklist

This is the most comprehensive documentation list you will need across the entire PT PMA setup process. Documents are categorized by whether they are required for all applicants, optional, or conditional on your specific business type.

Document Description Status
Deed of Establishment (Akta Pendirian) Notarial deed establishing the company, including articles of association. Must be drafted by a licensed Indonesian notary. Required
Ministry of Law Approval (SK Kemenkumham) Official approval letter from the Ministry of Law and Human Rights recognizing the company as a legal entity. Required
NIB (Nomor Induk Berusaha) Business Identification Number issued via the OSS RBA system. Functions as the company's primary business license and identity number. Required
NPWP (Tax Identification Number) Corporate tax ID number issued by the Directorate General of Taxes. Required for all tax filings and most banking operations. Required
Corporate Bank Account Indonesian corporate bank account in the PT PMA's name. Required for capital injection documentation and operational transactions. Required
Passport Copies (All Directors & Shareholders) Valid passport copies for all foreign directors and shareholders involved in the establishment. Required
KTP (for Indonesian Directors/Commissioners) National ID card of any Indonesian nationals serving as director, commissioner, or shareholder. Conditional
Investment Activity Plan (RPTKA) Required if the company intends to employ expatriate workers. Outlines planned foreign employee roles and justification. Conditional
Sector-Specific Business License Certain industries (food & beverage, health, finance, education) require additional licenses from the relevant ministry beyond the NIB. Conditional
IMTA (Work Permit for Expats) Work permit issued per expatriate employee. Required before a foreign national can legally work in Indonesia. Conditional
Environmental Compliance Document (UKL-UPL or AMDAL) Required for businesses that have potential environmental impact — manufacturing, construction, mining, large-scale F&B, etc. Conditional
Shareholder Agreement Not legally required but strongly recommended when there are multiple shareholders — especially in joint ventures with local partners. Optional
Local Virtual Office Agreement If using a virtual office for the registered address, a formal agreement with the virtual office provider is needed for documentation. Optional

✅ Pro Tip

All foreign documents submitted to Indonesian authorities must be apostilled in the country of origin and translated into Bahasa Indonesia by a sworn translator (penerjemah tersumpah). Skipping this step is one of the most common causes of application rejection and delays.

5 Step-by-Step Setup Timeline

Here is a realistic, phase-by-phase breakdown of the PT PMA establishment process from zero to operational. Timelines assume complete and correct documentation at each phase.

  1. 1

    Pre-Incorporation Planning

    1 – 2 Weeks

    Define business activities and KBLI codes, confirm foreign ownership eligibility for your sector via the Positive Investment List, determine shareholder and director structure, prepare source documents from home country (apostille + sworn translation), and secure a registered address in Indonesia.

  2. 2

    Notarial Deed & Ministry of Law Approval

    1 – 2 Weeks

    Work with a licensed Indonesian notary to draft and execute the Deed of Establishment (Akta Pendirian). The notary submits to the Ministry of Law and Human Rights (Kemenkumham) for formal legal entity recognition. This produces the SK Kemenkumham — the company's birth certificate.

  3. 3

    OSS RBA Registration & NIB Issuance

    3 – 7 Business Days

    Register the company on Indonesia's Online Single Submission Risk-Based Approach (OSS RBA) system at oss.go.id. Submit business details, KBLI codes, investment plan, and director information. Upon successful review, the system issues the NIB — the company's primary operating license and identity number.

  4. 4

    Tax Registration & NPWP Issuance

    3 – 5 Business Days

    Register the company with the local Tax Office (Kantor Pajak) to obtain the corporate NPWP. This is required before the company can open a bank account, sign contracts, or begin any taxable activity. Directors and commissioners may also need individual NPWPs.

  5. 5

    Corporate Bank Account Opening

    1 – 3 Weeks

    Open a corporate bank account at an Indonesian bank in the PT PMA's name. This is necessary for capital injection and all operational transactions. Major banks including BCA, Mandiri, BNI, and DBS Indonesia accept PT PMA accounts. Preparation of Know-Your-Customer (KYC) documents is required and timelines vary by bank.

  6. 6

    Sector-Specific Licenses (if applicable)

    2 – 8 Weeks

    Depending on your KBLI code and industry, you may need to obtain additional operating licenses from the relevant ministry — such as BPOM (food, health, cosmetics), OJK (financial services), or the Ministry of Communication (digital platforms). Timeline varies significantly by sector and license type.

  7. 7

    Operational Readiness

    Ongoing

    Set up payroll, BPJS registration for employees, monthly tax filing system, and any local employment contracts. At this stage, the company is fully legal and operational. First compliance obligations (monthly tax reports, BPJS contributions) begin from the first month of activity.

6 Tax & Financial Obligations

Tax compliance in Indonesia is non-negotiable and begins from the first month of operations. Foreign companies are often surprised by the frequency and granularity of Indonesian tax reporting requirements. Here is what to prepare for:

📅

Corporate Income Tax

Standard rate is 22% of taxable net income. Annual corporate tax return must be filed by the end of the 4th month following the fiscal year end. Monthly installments (PPh 25) are required throughout the year.

🧾

VAT (PPN)

11% VAT applies to most goods and services. Companies with annual revenue above IDR 4.8 billion must register as a Taxable Entrepreneur (PKP) and file monthly VAT returns (SPT Masa PPN).

👤

Employee Income Tax (PPh 21)

Employers must withhold income tax from employee salaries monthly and remit to the tax office. Monthly reporting is mandatory. Applies to both local and expatriate employees working in Indonesia.

💸

Withholding Tax (PPh 23 & 26)

Payments to Indonesian service providers are subject to PPh 23 (typically 2%). Payments to foreign parties (dividends, royalties, services) are subject to PPh 26 at 20%, reduced by applicable tax treaties.

📊

Transfer Pricing Rules

Transactions between your Indonesian PT PMA and related entities abroad must be conducted at arm's length and documented with a Transfer Pricing Document (TP Doc). Non-compliance triggers audits and significant penalties.

🌐

Tax Treaties

Indonesia has tax treaties with over 70 countries that can reduce withholding tax rates on dividends, interest, and royalties. Claiming treaty benefits requires a Certificate of Domicile (SKD) from your home country's tax authority.

💡 Recommended

Engage a local Indonesian tax consultant from day one — not just at year-end. Indonesian tax regulations change frequently, and monthly filing errors compound quickly. The cost of a local tax consultant is a fraction of the penalties that result from non-compliance.

7 Hiring & Employment Law

Indonesia has a detailed and employee-protective labor law framework under Law No. 13/2003 on Manpower (amended by the Omnibus Law in 2020). Foreign companies are often surprised by the obligations that apply from the moment they hire their first employee.

  • 📄

    Employment Contract Requirements

    All employees must have a written employment contract in Bahasa Indonesia (or bilingual). Contracts must specify role, compensation, working hours, and termination conditions. Fixed-term contracts (PKWT) are limited to a maximum of 5 years total and cannot be used for permanent or core business roles.

  • 🏥

    BPJS Registration (Mandatory Social Insurance)

    All employers must register employees with BPJS Kesehatan (healthcare) and BPJS Ketenagakerjaan (employment insurance). Combined employer-employee contribution rates range from approximately 6–10% of gross salary depending on position. Registration must be done within 30 days of hiring.

  • 💵

    Minimum Wage Compliance

    Indonesia sets minimum wages at the provincial and district level (UMP/UMK), updated annually. Jakarta's minimum wage is among the highest in the country. All employees must be paid at or above the applicable regional minimum wage — regardless of company size or stage.

  • ✈️

    Hiring Expatriates

    Foreign nationals working in Indonesia require an IMTA (Work Permit) and KITAS (Temporary Residence Permit). The employer must first obtain an RPTKA (Expatriate Manpower Utilization Plan) approved by the Ministry of Manpower. For each expatriate role, a local counterpart (Indonesian employee in a similar capacity) is often required to be trained for eventual knowledge transfer.

  • ⚖️

    Termination Rules

    Terminating an employee in Indonesia requires valid legal grounds and — in most cases — severance pay calculated based on years of service multiplied by one month's salary, plus additional long-service awards. Unilateral or improper termination can result in industrial relation disputes and mandatory reinstatement or enhanced severance awards.

8 Digital & E-Commerce Market Entry

For many foreign brands, Indonesia's digital commerce landscape is one of the primary reasons for market entry — and also one of the least understood. Indonesia has one of the highest social commerce adoption rates in the world, with TikTok Shop, Shopee, and Tokopedia forming the dominant commerce triangle.

What Foreign Brands Assume

  • A great product + a marketplace listing = sales
  • Indonesian consumers behave like Western e-commerce shoppers
  • Brand reputation from home country carries over automatically
  • Price competitiveness is the main decision driver
  • Digital ads alone can build market awareness
  • One marketplace is enough to reach the whole market

What Actually Works in Indonesia

  • Live commerce and creator endorsements drive the majority of conversions
  • Trust and social proof are built through people, not just products
  • Brand credibility must be built locally — from scratch
  • Value perception (quality + story) matters more than price alone
  • Native content with local creators outperforms all paid ad formats
  • A multi-platform strategy (TikTok + Shopee + Tokopedia) is the baseline

Livestream commerce in particular is not a trend in Indonesia — it is a fundamental commerce infrastructure. Platforms like TikTok Shop report that live sessions consistently generate 5–10x the conversion rates of standard product listings. Foreign brands that enter Indonesia without a live commerce strategy are entering at a significant structural disadvantage.

9 Common Mistakes Foreign Companies Make — and How to Avoid Them

The following mistakes are not hypothetical. They are patterns observed repeatedly among foreign companies entering Indonesia — each one avoidable with the right guidance upfront.

⚠️ Avoid These Costly Mistakes

  • Using a nominee shareholder arrangement — Having a local Indonesian "hold" shares on your behalf is illegal and puts you at serious legal and financial risk. If the nominee disputes ownership, Indonesian courts will side with the registered shareholder. Always use a legitimate PT PMA structure.
  • Choosing the wrong KBLI code — A KBLI code mismatch between your registered activities and what you actually do can invalidate your operating license and result in fines. Get this reviewed by a consultant before registering.
  • Ignoring monthly tax obligations — Indonesian tax reporting is monthly, not annual. Missing even one monthly filing triggers immediate penalties. Many foreign companies discover this too late, after penalties have accumulated.
  • Underestimating capital requirements — The IDR 10 billion investment threshold is not a suggestion. Undercapitalization is grounds for license revocation and creates complications with banking and future license applications.
  • Entering digitally without local content infrastructure — Launching on Shopee or TikTok Shop with translated copy and no local creator support almost never works. Indonesian consumers respond to culturally resonant, locally-hosted content — not repurposed global campaigns.
  • Skipping local legal counsel — Many foreign companies try to navigate the process using general information from the internet or their home-country legal team. Indonesian corporate law is specific, frequently updated, and requires local expertise to navigate correctly.
